## Summary

These workers install, program, maintain and repair wiring and equipment for burglar, access-control, video and fire alarm systems. They run low-voltage cable, mount panels, detectors and sirens, connect them, set them up, test them and show customers how they work. Most are employed by security-service firms and building-equipment contractors, and fire alarm work is tied to separate fire-protection standards.

## Path into the occupation

O*NET Job Zone 3: about 49% hold a post-secondary certificate and 41% a high school diploma (onetonline.org summary page). Training is mostly on the job or through a contractor, plus manufacturer courses. About 38 states license fire alarm technicians, and roughly 29 require or accept NICET fire alarm certification (four levels, from about 6 months to 10 years of experience); where a state has no program, NFPA 72 still points to third-party certification such as NICET (securityinfowatch.com article via search results, not opened in full). Requirements vary by state and by security versus fire work.

## Procedures and standard operating procedures

### P1. Electrically safe work for line-voltage connections

**Purpose:** Make the supply to a panel, power supply or transformer dead and prove it before touching it.

**When it is used:** Whenever a fire or security panel, power supply or other equipment is wired to or served from a line-voltage circuit.

**Who does it:** Installer (qualified person) with the facility electrician or owner's authorized employee.

**Steps**

1. Find the circuit that feeds the equipment and tell affected people the work is planned.
2. Switch off the circuit and open the disconnecting means; for fire alarm panels note that this removes protection, so arrange impairment steps first.
3. Apply a lock and tag to the disconnect, or use the equivalent measure if a lock cannot be fitted.
4. Release or restrain any stored energy, including backup batteries on the panel.
5. Test with a meter that works, check the circuit at the work point, then recheck the meter.
6. Do the work, keeping to required approach distances and using insulated tools.
7. Before restoring power, remove tools, check people are clear, remove locks and tags, then re-energize and confirm normal status.

**Hazards:** Shock from line-voltage terminals; Backfeed or induced voltage; Panel battery energy; Unexpected re-energizing; Loss of fire protection while the panel is off

**Controls:** Lock and tag; Meter verification before touching; Insulated tools and PPE chosen by hazard assessment; Qualified-person rule; Impairment notification when a fire system is off

**Standards**

- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.333: Deenergize live parts where possible, lock and tag the disconnect, verify dead with tests, check before re-energizing.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147: General lockout/tagout program for servicing machinery: written procedures, training, annual inspection.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132: Employer must assess hazards and supply and train workers on fitting PPE.
- NFPA 70E (copyrighted): Electrical safe work condition and arc-flash practices; applicability to low-voltage alarm panels is limited but the supply side counts.

**Evidence**

- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.333 (Lock and tag disconnects, verify de-energized state including backfeed, and inspect before re-energizing.)
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147 (Isolate, lock or tag, release stored energy and verify before servicing; annual procedure inspection.)
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132 (Hazard assessment and PPE training are employer duties.)

### P2. Plan reading and device layout check

**Purpose:** Work out where devices, panels and cable runs go and which circuits they use before pulling cable.

**When it is used:** At job start, from the work order, drawings and building plans.

**Who does it:** Installer or lead installer, with the designer or project manager.

**Steps**

1. Read the work order and scope, and confirm the system type (fire, intrusion, access control, video).
2. Check the approved shop drawings and device layout against the building plans.
3. Mark panel, power supply, device and annunciator locations, checking mounting and access space.
4. Plan cable routes, noting fire-rated walls, ceilings and spaces where firestopping or plenum cable is needed.
5. Identify each circuit's class (power-limited fire alarm, Class 2/3, non-power-limited, communications) and plan separation from power wiring.
6. List materials and quantities, and raise conflicts with the designer before drilling.

**Hazards:** Drilling into live wiring or structure; Wrong cable type in a plenum or fire-rated assembly; Using unapproved layout

**Controls:** Approved drawings on site; Scan walls before drilling; Cable type check against space; Layout reviewed with designer or inspector

**Standards**

- NFPA 72 (copyrighted): Sets device placement and spacing for fire alarm design; drawings are the basis for acceptance.
- NEC Article 760 / 725 / 800: Circuit class and cable type determine the wiring method and separation.

**Evidence**

- https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/49-2098.00 (Installers review work orders and blueprints to decide materials and installation methods.)
- https://www.mangoapps.com/templates/inspections/fire-alarm-acceptance-test-nfpa-72 (Acceptance testing expects approved shop drawings and a device layout to be on hand.)

### P3. Running low-voltage and fire alarm cable and raceway

**Purpose:** Route, support, separate and identify signaling cable so it stays safe and trouble-free.

**When it is used:** During rough-in, for new work and additions.

**Who does it:** Systems installer or technician, working to the plans and the employer's written procedure.

**Steps**

1. Choose cable of the right listed type for the circuit and the space (riser, plenum, general).
2. Keep fire alarm and Class 2/3 conductors out of enclosures and raceways with power or Class 1 conductors unless separated by a barrier or the approved exception applies.
3. Route through or along framing with the specified clearance or a protective plate.
4. Support cable with secure fastening at the required intervals and keep the work neat.
5. Firestop penetrations through fire-rated assemblies.
6. Label circuits at junction points so other work does not trigger signals, and remove abandoned cable.
7. Check the run for damage before termination.

**Hazards:** Cable damage or short against power conductors; Shock hazard if a fault crosses to line voltage; Spread of fire through penetrations; Falls from ladders

**Controls:** Listed cable types; Barrier or separation; Protective plates near framing; Firestopping; Circuit labels; Ladder safety

**Standards**

- NEC Article 760: Fire alarm circuits: power-limited and non-power-limited rules, separation, support and identification.
- NEC Articles 725 and 800: Class 2/3 and communications circuits: cable types and separation from power wiring.
- NEC 300.21 and 300.22: Fire-rated openings and ducts or plenum spaces where Article 760 refers to them.
- NFPA 72 (copyrighted): Circuit survivability and pathway class (supervision, survivability) for fire alarm wiring.

**Evidence**

- https://ecmweb.com/national-electrical-code/code-basics/article/55233196/nec-requirements-for-fire-alarm-systems (PLFA cable cannot share enclosures with power conductors without barrier; support, framing protection and circuit identification rules.)
- https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/49-2098.00 (Installers thread cable through walls, attics and cavities and install conduit.)

### P4. Installing panels, devices and power supply connections

**Purpose:** Mount and connect panels, detectors, notification devices and security devices, and supply the panel correctly.

**When it is used:** Trim-out after rough-in.

**Who does it:** Installer; a licensed electrician may do the line-voltage feed depending on the state.

**Steps**

1. Mount the panel and power supply at the planned location with working space and access.
2. Confirm the feed comes from a dedicated branch circuit with a marked disconnect and no GFCI or AFCI protection where the NEC forbids it.
3. Connect devices and terminate conductors, keeping circuit polarity and supervision correct.
4. Ground metal enclosures and connect equipment grounding conductors.
5. Install batteries last, only when ready to test.
6. Mount detectors, horns, strobes, pull stations and sensors at the layout locations.
7. Label the panel, circuits and the feed disconnect.

**Hazards:** Shock at line-voltage terminals; Battery short circuits; Wrong device on wrong circuit; Overhead work and ladders

**Controls:** Procedure 1 before work on the feed; Dedicated, marked fire alarm branch circuit; Equipment grounding; Insulated tools; Terminal checks

**Standards**

- NEC Article 760: Fire alarm branch circuit: dedicated, red-marked disconnect, restricted access, no GFCI or AFCI.
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.304: Wiring design: disconnecting means, grounding and identification of conductors.
- UL 864 and UL 681 (copyrighted): Product and installation listings for fire alarm control units and burglar alarm systems.
- NFPA 70 Article 110: Working space and workmanship rules where they apply to panels and enclosures.

**Evidence**

- https://ecmweb.com/national-electrical-code/code-basics/article/55233196/nec-requirements-for-fire-alarm-systems (PLFA supply: dedicated circuit, red disconnect marked FIRE ALARM CIRCUIT, restricted access, no GFCI or AFCI.)
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.304 (Disconnecting means, grounding and conductor identification apply to wiring design.)
- https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/49-2098.00 (Installers mount control panels, sensors and cameras and connect their wiring.)

### P5. Programming, addressing and sensitivity adjustment

**Purpose:** Make devices known to the panel and set them so they report correctly without false alarms.

**When it is used:** After device installation and before testing, and after changes.

**Who does it:** Installer or programming technician.

**Steps**

1. Assign addresses or zones to each device per the point list and drawings.
2. Enter device types, labels and zone text in the panel from the approved sequence of operations.
3. Set outputs, notification and monitoring communication settings.
4. Adjust detector sensitivity to the room within the allowed range.
5. Back up the programming and record the version.
6. Check that each point shows the correct label before functional testing.

**Hazards:** False alarms and nuisance signals; Wrong zone leading to wrong location reported; Lost programming

**Controls:** Point list checked against drawings; Backup of programming; Settings within listed range; Retest after any software change

**Standards**

- NFPA 72 (copyrighted): Reacceptance testing is needed after software changes and when devices are added or deleted.
- UL 864 (copyrighted): Listing covers control unit behavior and settings of listed panels.

**Evidence**

### P6. Functional and acceptance testing with documentation

**Purpose:** Show every device and the standby power work, and record the result for the authority having jurisdiction and owner.

**When it is used:** After installation and programming, and again after any added, deleted or modified components.

**Who does it:** Installer or certified technician, with the owner and the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) or inspector.

**Steps**

1. Confirm approved drawings, sequence of operations and the battery calculation are on hand.
2. Check wiring and insulation for faults and verify circuit supervision by creating open and ground conditions.
3. Test pull stations, smoke and heat detectors by approved methods, and supervisory inputs such as waterflow and tamper.
4. Verify audible and visible notification appliances operate across the coverage area.
5. Test standby battery condition and capacity and check the system survives the required standby period.
6. Confirm alarm, supervisory and trouble signals reach the panel, annunciators and monitoring service.
7. Record deficiencies and correct them, reset to normal, and obtain the inspector's sign-off.
8. Deliver records and train the owner.

**Hazards:** Loud notification appliances; False dispatch to the fire department; Shock from live equipment during testing; Ladders

**Controls:** Notify monitoring company and building occupants before testing; Hearing protection where needed; Written test record; AHJ witness where required

**Standards**

- NFPA 72 Chapter 14 (copyrighted): Acceptance, reacceptance and periodic inspection, testing and maintenance requirements, including tables of methods and intervals.
- Fire code (IFC or NFPA 1) and AHJ rules: Requires the system to be tested and maintained and sets who must witness or receive records.

**Evidence**

- https://www.mangoapps.com/templates/inspections/fire-alarm-acceptance-test-nfpa-72 (Acceptance covers device tests, notification, battery and standby duration, signals, deficiency list and inspector sign-off.)
- https://www.securitysales.com/insights/5_nfpa_codes_consider_maintaining_fire_alarm_sytems/896/ (NFPA 72 Chapter 14 covers testing and maintenance, with owners responsible for upkeep after acceptance.)

### P7. Impairment handling and restoration

**Purpose:** Control the risk and tell the right people when a fire alarm system or part is out of service.

**When it is used:** When a system is turned off for service, a fault takes devices out, or work lasts beyond a short period.

**Who does it:** Impairment coordinator (owner or designated person) with the service technician.

**Steps**

1. Notify the coordinator before the system is taken out of service and agree scope and duration.
2. Inform the fire department, owner, insurer and monitoring company as the plan requires.
3. Use a fire watch or other interim measures for longer outages.
4. Tag the affected equipment as out of service.
5. Complete the repair and run the tests needed to prove the affected parts work.
6. Notify all parties that protection is restored and remove the tag.

**Hazards:** Building left unprotected; Fire watch not in place; Restoration not tested

**Controls:** Impairment tag system; Fire watch; Notification to the fire department and others; Verification tests

**Standards**

- NFPA 72 Chapter 14 / NFPA 25 (copyrighted): Impairment coordinator duties, notification and restoration steps (NFPA 25 for sprinklers, 72 for fire alarm).
- Local fire code and insurer requirements: Set when the fire department must be told and when a fire watch is required.

**Evidence**

- https://www.bates.edu/environmental-health-safety/impairments (Coordinator verifies scope, notifies fire department and insurer, uses fire watch, tags equipment and confirms restoration.)
- https://www.securitysales.com/insights/5_nfpa_codes_consider_maintaining_fire_alarm_sytems/896/ (Fire codes place the maintenance obligation on owners, supported by an approved testing program.)

## Work the NEC does not govern

- Fire alarm design, device spacing, notification performance and acceptance, inspection and maintenance: NFPA 72, not the NEC.
- Product listing of panels and devices: UL 864 (fire) and UL 681 (burglar), plus central-station and monitoring standards such as UL 827.
- Fire code duties for owners, impairments, fire watch and AHJ witnessing: NFPA 1, International Fire Code, local amendments.
- Licensing, certification and permits: state and local rules, NICET.
- Programming, addressing and manufacturer instructions: vendor documentation.
- Security-specific work (cameras, access control, network setup) follows manufacturer instructions and often IT standards, with only the wiring under the NEC.
